A cold air return is the grille the furnace breathes through, and where it went
was decided by whoever framed the house. When it ends up somewhere that makes the
system worse — drawing off a hallway instead of the room it is meant to serve, or
short-circuiting a supply register a few feet away — the proper fix is sheet metal
and drywall. This is the printed version: a baffle that goes in behind the grille
and redirects the return, in an afternoon, for the cost of the filament.

**Read this before you download anything.** The folder ships Fusion source and a
STEP, and the STEP came out empty — it is 3.5 KB of product metadata with no
solid in it, which is why there is no STL here and nothing to spin in the
download section below. `bruh_cold_air_return_baffle_v2.f3d` is the real file.
You need Fusion, or something else that opens an `.f3d`, to get a printable part
out of this project.

That is less of a loss than it sounds, because you were going to have to resize
it anyway. Return openings are not standardised — a 14 × 20 return and a 12 × 12
return are both normal — and the duct throat behind the grille is smaller than
the grille's outer flange by an amount nobody agrees on. Measure yours, drive
the sketch off that number, export your own STL.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls / perimeters | 4 |
| Infill | 15% gyroid |
| Material | PLA+, or PETG for attic and near-furnace runs |
| Supports | Depends on how you orient your export — see below |

The perimeters matter more than the infill. A baffle is a flat plate standing in
moving air, and what stops it drumming is wall thickness, not what is inside it.
Four walls at 15% is quieter than two walls at 40%.

Orientation is yours to pick, because you are exporting the STL yourself: lay
the largest flat face on the bed, and the part will usually need no supports at
all. If it does, that is a sign to rotate rather than to turn supports on — a
support interface on the face that seals against sheet metal is a leak.

PLA+ is fine here in most houses. A return carries room air, not the hot supply
air coming off the heat exchanger, so the usual "PLA sags in a duct" warning does
not apply to the return side. It does apply if your return trunk runs through an
attic, where summer air alone will take a PLA part past its glass transition and
leave you with a curled baffle rattling around in the duct. Attic run, PETG.

## Sizing and fitting

<Steps>

1. **Take the grille off and measure the throat**, not the grille. What you want
   is the sheet-metal opening in the boot behind it and the depth available in
   front of any damper. Write down both, plus where the screws holding the
   grille on land, because those are the fixings you will reuse.

2. **Check what the grille is doing.** Some returns are filter-back — the grille
   hinges open and holds a 1 in filter. If yours is one of those, the baffle has
   to clear the filter frame entirely, and every millimetre of depth you take is
   a millimetre the filter no longer has.

3. **Open the `.f3d` and drive the sketch off your numbers.** Then export an STL
   and slice that. Keep the Fusion file; you will come back to it.

4. **Dry fit before you commit to anything.** Drop the baffle in with no tape and
   no screws, close the grille, and run the blower. You are checking two things:
   that the grille still sits flat, and that the return did not get louder.

5. **Mount it.** Screws through into the boot are the honest answer if it is
   staying. VHB is the answer if you are not sure yet — wipe the metal with
   isopropyl first, because thirty-year-old galvanised duct inside a wall is not
   a clean surface. Magnets in printed pockets are the answer if you expect to
   reprint it twice more.

6. **Seal the edges with foil tape** where the baffle meets the boot. Air takes
   the shortest path it can find, and an unsealed 2 mm gap along one edge is
   most of what you were trying to redirect.

</Steps>

## What will bite you

**Redirecting is fine. Narrowing is a whistle.** Returns in American houses are
routinely undersized to start with, and anything that cuts the free area of the
opening shows up immediately as noise at the grille and, less visibly, as a
blower working against more static pressure than it was sized for. Keep the free
area of the path through the baffle no smaller than the throat it sits in — turn
the air, do not pinch it. If the system got louder after you fitted it, that is
not the baffle bedding in. It is too tight, and it goes back in the slicer.

**It buzzes at one blower speed and not the others.** A thin printed plate in an
airstream has a resonance, and your furnace will find it. Add perimeters, or run
a strip of foil tape across the back face to damp it. The tape is the five-minute
version.

**It let go in the summer.** Either PLA in a hot run, or VHB on metal that was
not cleaned. Both are covered above and both are the same afternoon again.
