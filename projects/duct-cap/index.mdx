Round 4-inch duct is everywhere in a house — dryer, bath fan, range hood, the
booster fan somebody put in the attic — and the fittings for it come in exactly
the shapes a sheet-metal shop stocks and no others. These are the three parts
that fill the gap: a **collar** that clips onto the raw cut end of a pipe, a
**cap** that presses onto that collar and breathes through a honeycomb face
instead of sealing the duct shut, and a 61 mm **adapter** sleeve that steps
between a collared end and a plain one.

Nothing here is glued, threaded or screwed together. The whole set is held by
three diameters drawn a tenth of a millimetre apart, which is the good news and
the bad news at once — read the fits before you slice anything.

## What is actually in the STL

`4_duct_adapter_and_cap_41df03f3_v8.stl` is a Fusion design exported whole, not
a print plate. There are **five separate solids in it and only three of them are
parts**:

| Solid | What it is | Print it? |
|---|---|---|
| Cap | 110.4 mm skirt, honeycomb face, four pull lugs | Yes |
| Adapter | 61 mm stepped sleeve | Yes |
| Collar | 11 mm edge ring with an internal groove | Yes |
| Flat hex plate, 221 × 134 × 2 mm | The pattern body used to cut the honeycomb, left in the design | **No** |
| A push-to-connect union tee, 41.5 mm long | A manufacturer's CAD model linked into the design as a reference | **No** |

Split the STL to objects in your slicer and delete the last two before you do
anything else. The flat plate is a 221 mm sheet of hexagons that will happily
eat half a spool, and the fitting is a real pneumatic part with a steel grab
ring and an O-ring inside it — printing a copy gets you the shape, not a
fitting.

`quick_tee_v1.stl` is the same story at six sizes. It holds vendor models of
push-to-connect union tees for Ø4, Ø6, Ø8, Ø10, Ø12 and Ø16 mm tube, laid out in
a row 434 mm long, which is wider than most beds and is the first clue that it
is a reference file. Open it to lay out a run in CAD, or to model a cradle for a
fitting. Buy the fitting.

## The numbers that matter

Every dimension below is measured off the solids in the folder, not off a
drawing. The diameters are what the model says; what your printer does with them
is the last section of this page.

| Part | Bore | Outside | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cap | 104.5 mm | 110.4 mm skirt, 124.8 mm across the lugs | 25.3 mm |
| Adapter, small end | 102.0 mm | 106.0 mm | 61.0 mm overall |
| Adapter, large end | 104.8 mm | 110.4 mm | |
| Collar | 98.6 mm | 104.6 mm | 11.0 mm |

The collar is the piece that makes the set work. In section it is a **U-channel**
— two roughly 1 mm walls with a 0.95 mm slot between them, 7.2 mm deep, sitting
on a Ø101.4 circle. That circle is the wall of 4 in round duct, whose outside
diameter is 101.6 mm. Push the collar onto a cut pipe end and the raw sheet-metal
edge disappears into the slot, leaving an 11 mm long, 104.6 mm bead where there
used to be something that cuts you.

Everything else hangs off that bead:

- **Cap bore 104.5 on a 104.6 collar** — a tenth of interference, so the cap is a
  press fit. That is what the four lugs are for. They are 22 mm wide, stand 10 mm
  proud, are gusseted into the skirt, and they exist so you can get the cap back
  off.
- **Adapter large end 104.8 on the same bead** — two tenths of clearance, a slip
  fit.
- **Adapter small end 102.0 over bare 101.6 pipe** — four tenths, also a slip fit.

So the adapter joins a collared duct end to a plain one, and the cap closes off
whichever end you are not using.

The honeycomb face is 3.0 mm thick, with cells 8.7 mm across the flats on a
10.1 mm pitch and 1.4 mm webs, trimmed to a Ø103 opening. It stops leaves,
rodents and a hand. It does not stop insects and it is not a filter. Through the
middle runs a 12.2 mm bore tube, 15.0 mm outside, open at both ends over the
cap's full 25 mm depth — a pass-through for 12 mm OD tube or a fat cable, and
the reason there is a file of tees sitting next to this one.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls / perimeters | 3–4 |
| Infill | 20% gyroid |
| Material | PETG |
| Supports | None |
| Orientation | Cap face-down; adapter and collar standing on end |

All three print without supports in the orientation they export in. The cap goes
honeycomb-face down on the bed, which puts the skirt in the air and leaves the
lug gussets as 55° overhangs — steep enough to self-support, and the reason they
are chamfered at all.

The honeycomb webs are 1.4 mm, so at a 0.4 mm nozzle they come out as three or
four perimeters with nothing inside them. That is fine and it is what you want:
on that face the wall count is doing all the work and the infill percentage is
doing none.

:::caution
**Check the sliced preview of the collar before you print it.** It is two ~1 mm
walls with a 0.95 mm slot between them — three extrusion widths across a 3 mm
section. A slicer set to skip thin gaps will quietly fill the slot and hand you a
solid ring that goes onto nothing. If yours does, turn on thin-wall detection or
drop the wall width for that object until the preview shows the groove.
:::

## Fitting it

<Steps>

1. **Split the STL and delete the two solids that are not parts** — the flat
   221 mm hex plate and the pneumatic fitting. In Bambu Studio or PrusaSlicer
   that is right-click, Split, to objects.

2. **Print the collar first, on its own, and measure it.** You want 104.6 mm
   across the outside. That one 11 mm ring tells you everything about whether
   the other two parts will fit, and it costs ten minutes of filament to find
   out.

3. **Apply the correction to all three parts.** If the collar came out at
   104.2 mm, your printer is losing 0.4 mm on outside diameters and every
   diameter in the set is off by the same amount. Horizontal expansion, or XY
   hole compensation, is the setting to reach for; a uniform scale factor is the
   blunt version and works too.

4. **Push the collar onto the cut duct end.** It should go on with thumb
   pressure and stay there. Sheet-metal duct is thinner than the 0.95 mm groove,
   so if it rattles, run a lap of foil tape around the pipe end first and push
   the collar over that.

5. **Press the cap on.** A tenth of interference means it goes on firmly and
   comes off by the lugs — never by the honeycomb, which is how you snap a
   1.4 mm web.

6. **Tape both ends of the adapter.** Large end over the collar, small end over
   the plain pipe, then foil tape around each overlap. Add three self-tappers
   through the sleeve into the pipe underneath if the joint is somewhere you
   cannot get back to.

</Steps>

## What will bite you

**Never put this cap on a dryer exhaust termination.** A honeycomb face is a
screen, and the rule for dryer terminations is no screen at the outlet, for the
reason you would guess: lint packs into anything it can catch on, and a blocked
dryer duct is a fire. A backdraft damper is the part that belongs on that end of
a dryer run. Capped stubs, bath fans, intakes and anything moving clean air are
what this set is for.

**Nothing fits, and it is not the model's fault.** Every clearance here is
between 0.1 mm and 0.4 mm, which is smaller than the difference between two
printers, two filaments, or one filament in January and the same filament in
July. The design assumes you will measure and compensate. Step 2 exists because
printing the cheap part first is how you avoid printing the expensive part twice.

**The cap will not stay on.** The press fit is a tenth of a millimetre of
interference on a 104.5 mm bore, and one fat first layer eats all of it. Deburr
the collar, check the cap's first layer for elephant's foot, and if it is still
loose add about 0.15 mm of negative horizontal expansion to the cap alone.

**The run got louder, or weaker, after you fitted it.** The grille is a Ø103
opening that is roughly three-quarters open, so it is a restriction on any duct
that is actually moving air. It is a cap and a screen, not a diffuser. If there
is a fan behind it, put a proper grille on that end instead.

**It went brittle and cracked after a summer outside.** PLA and PETG both chalk
and embrittle under direct UV. Neither is a multi-year outdoor material; if the
cap terminates a run on a sunny wall, plan on reprinting it, or move to ASA and
an enclosed printer.
