Treats stack in a printed column with a closed floor. A servo pushes the bottom
one out through a gap in the side, the stack drops by one, and the next biscuit
is in position. That is the whole machine. It is worth building because it is the
rare automation with an audience — the dog learns within a week that the thing on
the wall is worth watching, and a button in Home Assistant becomes a way to say
hello to him from work.

:::note
**One part is in this folder, and it is the magazine.** `dog_treat_dispenser_1.stl`
is the column. The pusher, the servo mount and the catch tray were built around
the spot the dispenser lives in and never made it into a model file. So this page
documents the column properly — every dimension below is measured off the STL —
lists the parts that drive it, and says what the column's numbers tell you about
the mechanism you have to design. That is why it still carries the unfinished
flag.
:::

## What the printed part is

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Outside | 67 × 34 mm, corners rounded 2 mm |
| Length | 245 mm |
| Wall | 2.0 mm, uniform |
| Bore | 63 × 30 mm, sharp internal corners |
| Top | Fully open — the whole 63 × 30 bore |
| Base | Closed, 2.0 mm thick |
| Outlet | A 15 mm band above the base with the front and both sides missing |
| Window | 10 mm slot down the centre of one 67 mm face, 228 mm long |

**The part settles its own orientation.** One end is the bare 63 × 30 bore, wide
open, and that is the top — it is how you load it. The other end is a solid 2 mm
plate, and that is the floor a biscuit rests on. It does not run the other way up.

The bottom 17 mm is where the design is. Two of those millimetres are the floor;
the 15 mm above it has no front wall and no side walls, because one 67 mm face
runs the full 245 mm and everything else stops short. So the bottom biscuit in
the stack sits on a closed floor with three of its four sides open to the room.
Nothing slides across the bore here — a pusher reaches in through the 15 mm band
and shoves one treat out sideways, and the single wall that runs past the outlet
is the back the whole mechanism bolts to.

The window is the other thing the geometry tells you. It is 10 mm wide, dead
centre on the front face, and it runs from the top of the outlet clear off the
open end — 228 mm, so it leaves a 10 mm notch in the rim at the top. It is a
level gauge you can read from across a room and a slot you can get a finger into
when something bridges.

Measure your treats before you print 245 mm of tube, because nothing about the
bore adapts. They have to fall freely down 63 × 30 mm, and they have to leave
through a 15 mm gap — a biscuit thicker than that will not clear the outlet no
matter what pushes it. Hard, flat, uniform biscuits stack and slide; soft
training treats bridge across the bore and stay there. Filled to the rim it holds
about 460 cm³ of them.

- [Digital caliper](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000NEA0P8?tag=brau01-20) — measure the treats, then the bore, before the print starts

Buy the **metal-gear MG90S, not the plastic SG90**. A treat wedges against the
pusher sooner or later and the servo stalls trying to return; a plastic output
gear strips the first time that happens, and it always happens when nobody is
home.

Optional, and each one earns its place:

- [VL53L0X time-of-flight sensor](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D7LWDBVX?tag=brau01-20) — a 3-pack; point one down the tube from the open top and it reads the top of the stack in millimetres, which is a "treats remaining" number in Home Assistant
- [IR obstacle-avoidance modules](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01I57HIJ0?tag=brau01-20) — a 10-pack; one aimed across the outlet confirms a treat actually left, rather than assuming the servo moving meant something happened
- [Momentary push buttons](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07KX144DT?tag=brau01-20) — a 30-pack; somebody standing in front of the dispenser should not have to find their phone
- [Wyze Cam](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076H3SRXG?tag=brau01-20) — the point of dispensing a treat remotely is watching it land
- [PETG filament](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00ZAUR6M0?tag=brau01-20) — instead of PLA+ if you want to wash the column in hot water
- [M3 heat-set inserts](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08BCRZZS3?tag=brau01-20) — for the bracket and servo mount you design, **not** for the column: these are 5.7 mm long and the column's wall is 2.0 mm

If you would rather turn a drum or an auger than push a treat, the same servo
comes as a [continuous-rotation MG90S](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0925TDT2D?tag=brau01-20).
It gives you speed and direction instead of an angle, which means the firmware
has to time the dose rather than command a position — harder to get repeatable,
and nothing in this folder is built for it.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls | 5 |
| Top and bottom layers | 5 each |
| Infill | 0% |
| Supports | None lying flat; 17 mm of them standing up |
| Material | PLA+, or PETG if it will be washed hot |
| Orientation | Flat, back face on the bed |

Every wall in the part is exactly 2.0 mm, and 2.0 mm is both five 0.4 mm
perimeters and ten 0.2 mm layers. Set five walls, five top and five bottom solid
layers, and zero infill, and every wall comes out solid whichever way it happens
to be standing when it prints — no infill seam anywhere in the bore. That is
almost certainly why it was drawn at 2 mm.

**Lie it flat on the back face** — the unbroken one, opposite the window. It is
the only face that runs the whole 245 mm, so it puts 63 × 245 mm of plastic on
the bed and the part cannot tip. The one overhang is the front face, and the
window pays for itself there too: it splits that face into two 26.5 mm bridges
rather than one 63 mm span, which any printer crosses cleanly.

That wants a 245 mm bed. A 250 × 210 Prusa or a 256 mm Bambu takes it lengthways;
a 220 × 220 Ender does not, at any angle — rotated 45° it still needs 220.6 mm.
On a 220 mm bed, stand it on the closed base instead. It is 245 mm tall that way,
which clears a 250 mm Z axis by five millimetres, and it needs supports for the
first 17 mm only: the front and both side walls begin in mid-air where the outlet
band ends. Three short support columns, nothing above them.

Don't scale it to get a taller magazine. It is not a plain prism — stretching the
long axis stretches the 2 mm base and the 15 mm outlet with it, and a 20 mm
outlet lets two treats out at once. Cut it instead, anywhere in the parallel
section between the outlet and the top.

## Wiring

There is no firmware in this folder, so there are no pin numbers to quote — you
pick the GPIO. What is worth writing down is the servo's lead colours, because
they are the thing people get backwards:

| Servo lead | Goes to |
|---|---|
| Brown | GND |
| Red | 5V |
| Orange | The GPIO you chose |

Run the red lead to the 5V rail feeding the board, not to the board's 3.3V pin.
An MG90S pulls close to an amp when it stalls, and a 3.3V regulator asked for
that is a board that reboots every time it hands out a treat.

## Assembly

<Steps>

1. **Deburr the bore and the outlet**, before anything else. The three cut edges
   around the outlet band are what the bottom biscuit drags across on its way out,
   and a printed edge left as printed will catch it.

2. **Dry-stack the treats.** Load the column by hand, stand it up, and watch where
   they hang up on the way down. Fix that with sandpaper now, not after the servo
   is mounted.

3. **Build the pusher to the 15 mm band.** It has to sweep the bottom treat clear
   of the column and be back before the one above has dropped far enough to
   follow — how far that is depends entirely on your treats, which is what step 2
   was for. Come in from one 30 mm side and out the 63 mm front, or straight out
   the front; all three faces are open to you.

4. **Bolt the servo to the back face, M3 and a nut.** That face is the only wall
   that runs past the outlet, so it is the only thing at the bottom of the column
   there is to fix to. It is 2 mm thick: it will not hold a heat-set insert, and
   it will not hold a self-tapper for long.

5. **Mount the column vertically**, closed base down, window facing where you can
   see it. It is a 245 mm lever with treats in it, so bracket it near the top as
   well as at the base.

6. **Test with the column empty**, then with three treats, then full. A pusher
   that works empty can stall under the weight of a full column.

</Steps>

## Firmware

Write this one yourself; there is no config in the folder to copy. In ESPHome it
is three pieces: a `servo` component on a PWM output — `ledc` on an ESP32,
`esp8266_pwm` on an ESP8266 — a `script` that moves it to the open angle, waits,
and moves it back, and a `button` that runs the script. The button is what
appears in Home Assistant, and `button.press` in an automation is then the whole
integration — a schedule, a voice command or a card on the dashboard all do the
same thing.

Two things to get right. Detach the servo between presses, or it will hum against
the pusher all day and cook itself. And put the two angles in `substitutions` at
the top of the file, because those are the numbers you will change ten times
while tuning step 3.

If you added the VL53L0X, it is the `vl53l0x` platform on I²C and it reports a
distance; a `calibrate_linear` block turns that into a treat count, and a
template binary sensor turns the count into "the dispenser is empty" — which is
the automation actually worth having, because a dispenser everyone believes is
loaded and isn't teaches the dog to stop checking.

## Keeping it clean

:::caution
A 3D-printed part is not food-safe in the strict sense. Layer lines hold crumbs,
crumbs hold everything else, and no filament sold as food-safe stays that way
once a 0.4 mm nozzle has drawn grooves down the inside of it. Dry biscuits only —
no wet food, nothing greasy. Empty the column and wipe the bore out monthly.
:::

That is also the argument for printing it in PETG rather than PLA+. PETG takes
hot soapy water without going soft; PLA+ starts to sag around 60 °C, which is
well inside what a tap can do. Neither belongs in a dishwasher, but only one of
them survives a rinse you weren't paying attention to.
