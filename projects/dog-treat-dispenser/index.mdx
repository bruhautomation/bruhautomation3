Treats stack in a printed column, a servo pulls a slide at the bottom, one
biscuit falls out, the slide shuts. That is the whole machine. It is worth
building because it is the rare automation with an audience — the dog learns
within a week that the thing on the wall is worth watching, and a button in
Home Assistant becomes a way to say hello to him from work.

:::note
**One part is in this folder, and it is the column.** `dog_treat_dispenser_1.stl`
is the magazine tube. The gate, the servo mount and the chute underneath it were
built around the spot the dispenser lives in and never made it into a model file.
So this page documents the column properly — every dimension below is measured
off the STL — lists the parts that drive it, and says what the column's numbers
tell you about the gate you have to design. That is why it still carries the
unfinished flag.
:::

## What the printed part is

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Outside | 67 × 34 mm, corners rounded 2 mm |
| Length | 245 mm, open at both ends |
| Wall | 2.0 mm, uniform |
| Bore | 63 × 30 mm, sharp internal corners |
| Slot | 10 mm wide, cut clean through one 67 mm face |
| Slot length | 228 mm from one end, leaving 17 mm of unbroken tube at the other |

Those last two rows are the ones that tell you how it was meant to work. The
17 mm of unbroken tube goes at the **bottom**: it is the only stretch with four
complete walls, which is what a sliding gate needs to run against. Everything
above it is a loading window — you can see the stack, you can drop treats in
without lining anything up, and you can reach a finger in when one jams.

Measure your treats before you print 245 mm of tube. The bore is 63 × 30 mm and
nothing about it adapts. Hard, flat, uniform biscuits stack and slide; soft
training treats bridge across the bore and stay there.

Buy the **metal-gear MG90S, not the plastic SG90**. A treat wedges against the
gate sooner or later and the servo stalls trying to close; a plastic output gear
strips the first time that happens, and it always happens when nobody is home.

- [IR obstacle-avoidance modules](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01I57HIJ0?tag=brau01-20) — a 10-pack; one aimed across the outlet confirms a treat actually left, rather than assuming the servo moving meant something happened
- [Momentary push buttons](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07KX144DT?tag=brau01-20) — a 30-pack; somebody standing in front of the dispenser should not have to find their phone
- [Wyze Cam](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076H3SRXG?tag=brau01-20) — the point of dispensing a treat remotely is watching it land
- [PETG filament](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00ZAUR6M0?tag=brau01-20) — instead of PLA+ if you want to wash the column in hot water
- [M3 heat-set inserts](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08BCRZZS3?tag=brau01-20) — for the bracket and servo mount you design, **not** for the column: these are 5.7 mm long and the column's wall is 2.0 mm

If you would rather turn a drum or an auger than slide a gate, the same servo
comes as a [continuous-rotation MG90S](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0925TDT2D?tag=brau01-20).
It gives you speed and direction instead of an angle, which means the firmware
has to time the dose rather than command a position — harder to get repeatable,
and nothing in this folder is built for it.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm, or 0.28 mm — there is no detail to lose |
| Walls | 5 |
| Infill | 0% |
| Supports | None |
| Brim | Yes |
| Material | PLA+, or PETG if it will be washed hot |
| Orientation | Standing on end |

The wall is exactly 2.0 mm, which is five 0.4 mm extrusions. Set five walls and
zero infill and the whole part comes out as solid perimeters with no infill seam
anywhere in the bore — that is almost certainly why it was drawn at 2 mm.

**Stand it on end.** Lying flat, the inside of the upper wall becomes a 63 mm
bridge, and a rough bridge is exactly where a biscuit stops sliding. Standing up
there are no overhangs at all, and the layer lines run around the tube rather
than along it. It is 245 mm tall that way, which clears a 250 mm Z axis by five
millimetres and nothing more, so skip the raft.

The bed contact standing up is only the 2 mm ring — about 365 mm² holding up a
245 mm tower. Use a brim, and don't put it on the corner of the bed where a
draught can get at it.

If you want a shorter or longer magazine, scale **only the long axis**. It is a
straight prism, so stretching it lengthways changes nothing else. Scaling the
other two axes changes the wall thickness and the slot width at the same time,
and then five walls no longer add up to the wall.

## Wiring

There is no firmware in this folder, so there are no pin numbers to quote — you
pick the GPIO. What is worth writing down is the servo's lead colours, because
they are the thing people get backwards:

| Servo lead | Goes to |
|---|---|
| Brown | GND |
| Red | 5V |
| Orange | The GPIO you chose |

Run the red lead to the 5V rail feeding the board, not to the board's 3.3V pin.
An MG90S pulls close to an amp when it stalls, and a 3.3V regulator asked for
that is a board that reboots every time it hands out a treat.

## Assembly

<Steps>

1. **Deburr the bore**, both ends, before anything else. The bottom opening is
   the one that matters — treats leave through it.

2. **Dry-stack the treats.** Load the column by hand, tip it, and watch where
   they hang up. Fix that with sandpaper now, not after the gate is glued on.

3. **Build the gate into the bottom 17 mm.** That stretch has four unbroken
   walls and the slot starts immediately above it. The gate wants to open far
   enough to release one treat and close before the next has fallen far enough to
   follow — how far that is depends entirely on your treats, which is what step 2
   was for.

4. **Bolt the servo through, M3 and a nut.** The wall is 2 mm: it will not hold a
   heat-set insert, and it will not hold a self-tapper for long.

5. **Mount the column vertically**, unbroken end down, slot facing where you can
   see it. It is a 245 mm lever with treats in it, so bracket it near the top as
   well as the bottom.

6. **Test with the column empty**, then with three treats, then full. A gate that
   works empty can jam under the weight of a full column.

</Steps>

## Firmware

Write this one yourself; there is no config in the folder to copy. In ESPHome it
is three pieces: a `servo` component on a `ledc` output for the gate, a `script`
that moves it to the open angle, waits, and moves it back, and a `button` that
runs the script. The button is what appears in Home Assistant, and `button.press`
in an automation is then the whole integration — a schedule, a voice command or a
card on the dashboard all do the same thing.

Two things to get right. Detach the servo between presses, or it will hum against
the gate all day and cook itself. And put the open and closed angles in
`substitutions` at the top of the file, because those are the two numbers you
will change ten times while tuning step 3.

If you added the VL53L0X, it is the `vl53l0x` platform on I²C and it reports a
distance; a `calibrate_linear` block turns that into a treat count, and a
template binary sensor turns the count into "the dispenser is empty" — which is
the automation actually worth having, because a dispenser everyone believes is
loaded and isn't teaches the dog to stop checking.

## Keeping it clean

:::caution
A 3D-printed part is not food-safe in the strict sense. Layer lines hold crumbs,
crumbs hold everything else, and no filament sold as food-safe stays that way
once a 0.4 mm nozzle has drawn grooves down the inside of it. Dry biscuits only —
no wet food, nothing greasy. Empty the column and wipe the bore out monthly.
:::

That is also the argument for printing it in PETG rather than PLA+. PETG takes
hot soapy water without going soft; PLA+ starts to sag around 60 °C, which is
well inside what a tap can do. Neither belongs in a dishwasher, but only one of
them survives a rinse you weren't paying attention to.
