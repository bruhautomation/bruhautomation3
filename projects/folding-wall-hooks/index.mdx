A rack of hooks is useful about a third of the time and in the way for the rest
of it — every empty hook is still a thing to catch a shoulder on in a hallway.
This is a slat panel where the hooks live *inside* the gaps between the boards.
Pull one down and you have a four-inch peg. Flick it up and the wall is flat
again.

Sixteen of them, in two rows of eight, all pivoting on two lengths of
quarter-inch steel rod that run the full width of the panel behind the slat
faces. It is a lumber build with printed hooks, not a printed panel — nothing in
it is beyond a saw and a drill.

## How it works

The hook is a square-section bar with a big rounded heel at one end, bored on
its centreline for the pivot rod. It swings through exactly 60°, and both end
positions are set by geometry rather than by a detent.

**Folded**, the bar stands dead vertical in the gap between two slats. Its back
is a flat plane that lands against the backer rail behind the slats, and the
pivot sits on the bar's centreline, so it is balanced over its own axis with its
back leaning on the rail. Nothing latches it. Friction in the bore is what keeps
it there.

**Deployed**, a flat on the heel comes round and lands on that same rail, and
the bar stops at 30° above horizontal.

In between, the heel *lifts off*. Rotate the profile about the pivot and the
rearmost point of the hook never goes deeper than where it started — it backs
away from the rail by up to 1.4 mm halfway through the swing and settles onto it
again at the far end. The rail needs no relief cut, no notch and no clearance
pocket. It is a flat board.

The clever part is the tip. The last 34 mm of the bar is a single plane raked
30° off the bar's own axis, so when the bar rotates 60° into the deployed
position that plane ends up **dead level**. The peg is a 30° ramp for most of
its length with a flat landing at the outer end: anything you hang slides inward
towards the wall and then stops, and there is no lip on the end to snag a coat
loop on when you take it off.

## The panel

Every number here is measured off the STEP file in this folder. The whole thing
is drawn to nominal 1×2 dimensions.

| Part | Size | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Slat | 1-1/2 × 3/4 × 12 in (38.1 × 19.05 × 304.8 mm) | 9 |
| End stile | same stock, turned on edge | 2 |
| Backer rail | 19-1/2 × 1-1/4 × 3/4 in (495.3 × 31.75 × 18.8 mm) | 4 |
| Pivot rod | 1/4 in dia × 21 in (6.35 × 533.4 mm) | 2 |
| Printed hook | see below | up to 16 |

The slats sit on **2-1/4 in (57.15 mm) centres**, which leaves eight gaps of
exactly 3/4 in. The gap is the hook pocket, which is why the slat spacing is not
negotiable. The two stiles are the same stock turned on edge and sit flush
against the outermost slats, no gap either side.

Overall the panel is **21 × 13 in and stands 1-1/2 in off the wall**. The slats
and the stiles are all 12 in long, but they are offset from each other by
exactly an inch — the stiles sit an inch higher, so they stand proud at the top
and the slat ends hang below the bottom rail as a fringe. That inch is not
decoration; see the last section.

Here is every elevation in the panel, measured from the bottom end of a slat,
which is the lowest point of the whole thing:

| Feature | Height |
|---|---|
| Slat, bottom to top | 0 → 304.8 mm |
| Stile, bottom to top | 25.4 → 330.2 mm |
| Rail 1 | 25.4 → 57.2 mm |
| **Lower rod centre** | **41.9 mm** |
| Rail 2 | 105.8 → 137.6 mm |
| Rail 3 | 192.6 → 224.4 mm |
| **Upper rod centre** | **207.0 mm** |
| Rail 4 | 273.1 → 304.8 mm |

The two rods are **165.1 mm (6-1/2 in) apart** and each runs at the mid-height
of a rail — rail 1 and rail 3. That pairing is the whole mechanism: the rail is
what the hook's heel bears against at both ends of its swing, so a rod without a
rail behind it has no stop. Rails 2 and 4 carry no rod and exist only to glue
slats to.

:::note
**The rails are never drilled.** The rods pass through the nine slats and the
two stiles only, at 9.65 mm back from the front face. The rails sit behind that,
starting 19.3 mm back. Eleven pieces get holes, four pieces do not.
:::

## The hook

One printed part, sixteen times. It is a straight extrusion of a 2D profile, so
there is no overhang anywhere in it.

| Feature | Dimension |
|---|---|
| Width, along the pivot axis | 19.05 mm — the same 3/4 in as the slat gap |
| Bar section | 19.30 mm across, parallel for 84.9 mm |
| Length, pivot centre to tip | 120.0 mm |
| Pivot centre | on the bar centreline, 15.6 mm up from the heel |
| Overall, folded | 19.3 × 135.3 mm |
| Overall envelope, deployed | 117.5 × 68.9 mm |
| Reach in front of the slat face | 98.4 mm (3-7/8 in) |
| Level landing at the tip | 33.9 mm |
| Tip | tapers to 2.5 mm, rounded on a 1.27 mm radius |
| Part volume | 42.8 cm³ |

The pivot is at 9.65 mm because that is half the bar's own 19.30 mm section, and
the hook's front face sits flush with the front of the slats. It lands a tenth
of a millimetre behind the slats' actual mid-thickness. Nobody has ever noticed.

Printed the way I would print it — five walls, 30% infill — that is a bit over
30 g each, so a full set of sixteen is roughly half a kilogram of filament. Buy
the spool before you start, not after hook eleven.

## Getting a printable hook out of the file

:::caution
The STL in this folder is the **whole assembly** — nine slats, two stiles, four
rails, two rods and sixteen hooks in one mesh, 33 bodies. Sending it to a slicer
as-is will not do anything useful. And the rod is drawn passing straight through
solid hooks, so **there is no pivot bore in the model.** You have to add one.
:::

Two ways round it, and the second is better.

Load the STL into PrusaSlicer, Orca or Bambu Studio and use **split to objects**.
Delete everything but one hook, print sixteen of it, and drill the pivot hole
afterwards. Workable, but boring 19 mm of printed plastic with a 1/4 in bit
wants a slow speed and a pecking action or the bit grabs and melts a spiral out
of it.

Or open `folding_wall_hooks_v4.f3d` (or the STEP), delete everything except one
hook, cut a **6.6 mm hole** on the pivot centre — 9.65 mm from each face,
15.6 mm up from the heel end — and export that. Fifteen minutes, and every hook
after it comes out right. The extra 0.25 mm over the rod's nominal 6.35 mm is
what makes it turn rather than seize, and a printed hole comes out undersize
anyway, so expect to run a round needle file through it once.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls / perimeters | 5 |
| Infill | 30% |
| Supports | None |
| Material | PLA+ indoors, PETG anywhere warm |
| Orientation | Profile flat on the bed, 19.05 mm tall |

**Orientation is the one thing worth getting right.** Lay the profile flat so
the part is 19.05 mm tall and the pivot bore runs straight up and down. The
layers then stack along the pivot axis, which means a coat hanging off the tip
loads the extrusions along their length and never pulls a layer apart. Stand the
hook up on end instead and you have built a cantilever out of stacked layer
joints — it will snap at the heel the first time somebody hangs a wet coat on
it.

Wall count is doing the work here, not infill. The bending stress is all in the
outer skin at the heel, so five perimeters on a 0.4 mm nozzle is worth more than
turning the infill up.

Side loads are the ones that will break it. Someone leaning on a deployed hook,
or a bag swinging sideways into it, puts the load across the layers instead of
along them. That is the direction the part is weakest in, and no slicer setting
fixes it.

## Drilling

Twenty-two holes, and they have to agree with each other. If the eleven holes on
one rod line are not collinear the rod will not go through at all; if they are
nearly-but-not-quite collinear the rod goes in bent and every hook on it binds.

Two numbers do all the work. **Every hole is 9.65 mm back from the front face**
of its piece, and every hole is at one of two heights.

| Piece | Lower hole | Upper hole |
|---|---|---|
| Slat ×9 | 41.9 mm from the bottom end | 207.0 mm from the bottom end |
| Stile ×2 | 16.5 mm from the bottom end | 181.6 mm from the bottom end |

:::caution
**The stiles are not drilled at the same heights as the slats.** All eleven
pieces are 12 in long, but the stiles sit an inch higher in the finished panel,
so their holes are 25.4 mm lower relative to their own ends. Mark all eleven
pieces laid out in the position they will end up in — stiles an inch proud at
the top — and the two rod heights become one straight line across the lot.
Square them all off flush and drill one set of heights and you will have built
two pieces that cannot go on.
:::

<Steps>

1. **Cut all eleven pieces to 12 in** and square the ends. Every measurement
   below is off an end, so an end that is not square is an error you cannot
   correct later.

2. **Do not try to stack-drill them.** Nine slats face to face is 13-1/2 in of
   wood and there is no quarter-inch bit that long. Each piece only needs a hole
   through 38.1 mm of slat, or 19.05 mm of stile, which is nothing.

3. **Set up a fence and a stop instead.** Lay the piece down so the hole runs
   vertically, clamp a straight fence 9.65 mm from the bit centre with the front
   face against it, and clamp a stop block at the first hole height. Run all nine
   slats. Move the stop, run all nine again. Then move it 25.4 mm for the two
   stiles and do both heights again.

4. **No drill press? Make a bushing block.** Drill one offcut of the same stock
   accurately, then clamp it on top of each piece as a guide and follow it with
   the bit. A wood guide block keeps a hand drill honest over 38 mm far better
   than an eye does.

5. **Check the exit face on the first one.** A bit that is 2° off looks fine
   going in and is 1.3 mm out by the time it comes through the far side. Find
   that on slat one, not on slat nine.

</Steps>

The rod is 21 in long — exactly the panel width — so it ends flush with the
outside face of each stile and is captive once the frame is together. Nothing
retains it and nothing needs to.

## Assembly

<Steps>

1. **Build the ladder frame.** Four rails between the two stiles at the heights
   in the table above, pocket screws from behind, corner clamps holding it
   square. Check the diagonals before the glue grabs — a frame out of square puts
   the two rods out of parallel, and the hooks will tell you about it.

2. **Dry-fit the slats** on the front of the rails at 2-1/4 in centres, sitting
   an inch lower than the stiles. A 3/4 in offcut is the perfect spacer: drop it
   in each gap, push the next slat up to it, pull it out.

3. **Thread the lower rod** through the stile, then alternate slat, hook, slat,
   hook all the way across and out through the far stile. Do this **before** any
   slat is glued down. There is no way to fit a hook afterwards.

4. **Glue the slats to the rails** with the rod in place holding the spacing.
   2P-10 on the rails, activator on the slats, press and hold for ten seconds
   each.

5. **Thread the upper rod** the same way and check every hook swings its full
   60° without touching the slat either side of it.

6. **Finish it** before it goes on the wall — the gaps are impossible to get
   into once the panel is vertical. Oil the hooks too; it costs nothing and stops
   the printed faces squeaking on the wood.

7. **Hang it** on two screws through the top rail and two through the bottom,
   into studs where you can find them and anchors where you cannot. The load
   pulls the panel off the wall at the top, so the top pair are the ones that
   matter.

</Steps>

## Two things that will bite you

**The hooks are exactly as wide as the gaps.** 19.05 mm of hook in a 19.05 mm
slot is zero clearance, and a printed part is always a touch wider than nominal —
especially on the first layer, which here is one of the two faces that has to
slide. Sand two or three strokes off each face of every hook before assembly, or
scale the part to 98% along the pivot axis in the slicer. Do it before the panel
is glued up, not after.

**Bore fit is what holds a folded hook up.** There is no catch, no magnet and no
spring — the bar simply balances over its pivot with its back against the rail.
A bore that turns sweetly on the rod stays where you put it. A sloppy one lets
the hook drift open on its own, and you are back to a wall covered in pegs. Aim
for a hook you can turn with one finger but that will not fall under its own
weight, and file up to it rather than drilling straight to a number.

One more, less a trap than the reason for that inch of offset: when the top row
folds up, the hook tips land **22 mm above the slats and 3.5 mm shy of the top of
the stiles** — right in the notch the offset creates. That is your finger grip,
and it is why the stiles stand proud. The bottom row's tips finish buried inside
the slot, so they need a fingernail to start them. Worth knowing when you decide
which row gets the things you put up and take down every day.
