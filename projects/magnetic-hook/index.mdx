A hook you can move. It threads onto the M6 stud of a rubber-coated neodymium
magnet, so it sticks to anything ferrous — a steel garage door, the side of a
tool chest, a rack upright, the underside of a shelf — and comes off again
without leaving a hole or a patch of adhesive residue. There are two parts to
buy and one to print.

The shape is the reason to build this one rather than buy a hook: it is a
**closed** hook. The arm goes out, arches over, and comes back to within 27 mm of
the mounting face. Whatever you hang on it can be knocked, swung and driven over
a speed bump, and it stays on.

## The shape

Every number here is measured off `bruh_mag_hook_v2.step` in this folder.

| Feature | Dimension |
|---|---|
| Overall | 57.5 × 15 × 60 mm |
| Stands off the mounting face | 60 mm |
| Mounting pad | 15 × 15 mm |
| Arm section | 15 × 15 mm square |
| Stud bore | 6.0 mm dia, 16 mm deep, blind |
| Nut pocket | 10.0 mm across flats, 6 mm deep, side entry |
| Throat, clear | 32.5 mm across × 50 mm deep |
| Mouth | 27 mm |
| Arch radius | 15.5 mm outside, 5 mm on every inside corner |
| Model volume | 21.1 cm³ |

The load-bearing arm is the one with the magnet on the end of it: a 15 mm square
bar that stands straight out of the mounting face for 45 mm. Everything else —
the arch and the short returning leg — exists to stop things leaving.

**Mount it with the magnet at the bottom and the arch above.** Then the square
arm is a 45 mm ledge with the wall as its back stop, the returning leg is a roof
over it, and the only way out is up 32.5 mm and back through the 27 mm mouth.
Flip it over and everything you hang dangles off the far end of a 60 mm
cantilever, which is the worst possible way to load a magnet. On a horizontal
surface overhead it works the obvious way round: the arm hangs down, the arch is
the bottom of the hook, and the return leg keeps the load from swinging out.

## Parts

- [M6 stud magnet base, 40 lb, rubber-coated](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07JLKXRV6?tag=brau01-20) — the part the whole thing is drawn around. The rubber matters twice over: it won't mark a painted door, and friction is what gives a magnet any useful grip in shear. Four in a pack, so print four hooks
- [M6 hex nut](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=M6+hex+nut+DIN+934+stainless&tag=brau01-20) — one per hook, 10 mm across flats, plain or nyloc. The pocket is 6 mm deep, so a 5 mm standard nut has a millimetre of slop in it and a 6 mm nyloc fills it exactly
- [PLA+ filament, 1.75 mm](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CJ2LB5FZ?tag=brau01-20) — the default, and about 20 g a hook, so a spool is fifty of them
- [PETG filament, 1.75 mm](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00ZAUR6M0?tag=brau01-20) — use this instead in a garage, a shed or a car. A loaded hook is a permanent load, and PLA creeps under one, faster when it's warm
- [Blue threadlocker](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000I1RSNS?tag=brau01-20) — one drop on the stud. Anything that gets bumped daily unscrews itself eventually
- [Twist drill set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07DH55YRX?tag=brau01-20) — the bore wants reaming, see below
- [Deburring tool](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09ND84SXF?tag=brau01-20) and a [needle file set](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B094FFQ8QT?tag=brau01-20) — for the bore mouth and the nut pocket
- [Digital caliper](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000NEA0P8?tag=brau01-20) — measure your nut across the flats before you print four hooks it doesn't fit

## Before you slice it

:::caution
The STL carries a stray block. There is a solid 10 × 5.4 × 5 mm slab sitting on
the side face directly over the mouth of the nut slot — the tool body that cut
the slot, left in the export. Print the file as it stands and the slot is walled
off, and the nut has nowhere to go.
:::

It is attached only around the rim of the slot opening, so there are two ways
past it and both are quick. In the slicer, drop a **negative volume** over that
corner of the part and it disappears from the sliced output. Or open
`bruh_mag_hook_v2.f3d` or the STEP, delete the body called `Body3`, and export a
clean mesh. Cutting it off after printing works too, but the block sits exactly
where the part has to meet the bed, so you would be dressing a face you then have
to print on top of — delete it first.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls / perimeters | 5 |
| Infill | 40% |
| Supports | None |
| Material | PLA+ indoors, PETG anywhere warm |
| Orientation | On the face the nut slot opens through, 15 mm tall |

**Lay it down on the slotted face.** The part is then 15 mm tall, the whole
outline becomes a straight extrusion, and there is not a single overhang in it —
no supports, anywhere. It was drawn to print this way: the nut pocket comes out
as an open-bottomed 10 mm slot with a 30° peak on top, which steps in about a
third of a millimetre per layer and needs no bridge. Stand the part on its base
instead and the returning leg starts in mid-air, 27 mm up.

The other reason is strength. Flat on that face, the layers stack across the
15 mm width and every extrusion runs the length of the arch, so a load on the
ledge pulls along them. Printed upright, the same load is trying to peel apart
layer joints at the root of the arm — which is also where the nut pocket has
already taken a 10 × 6 mm bite out of a 15 × 15 mm section. That is the
highest-stress spot on the part and it is not where you want a glue line.

Walls do the work, not infill. Five perimeters on a 0.4 mm nozzle puts nearly
4 mm of solid shell across a 15 mm section; the 40% is mostly there to hold the
bore round.

## Assembly

<Steps>

1. **Ream the bore.** It is drawn at exactly 6.0 mm and an M6 stud is exactly
   6.0 mm, so there is no clearance in the model at all — and printed on its side
   the top of the hole always comes out a little flat. Run a 1/4 in (6.35 mm) bit
   through it by hand. A drill on a trigger will grab and spiral the plastic out.

2. **Check the nut goes in.** The pocket is 10.0 mm across flats and so is the
   nut. If it will not push in under thumb pressure, two strokes of a flat needle
   file on each pocket wall gets you there. Do not force it — those two walls are
   the only thing stopping the nut spinning.

3. **Slide the nut in** from the slot side until it sits up under the peak.
   Nothing retains it sideways, so if the hook is going somewhere it will be
   handled a lot, one drop of CA in the slot once the nut is seated settles that
   permanently.

4. **A drop of threadlocker on the magnet's stud**, then thread the hook down
   onto it and nip it up by hand. Hand tight is the whole spec: the nut is steel
   and the pocket is plastic, and you are only holding a hook.

5. **Aim it after it is tight.** The hook stops wherever the thread runs out,
   which will not be where you wanted it pointing. Do not fight it — stick the
   magnet to the steel and twist the whole assembly on the surface until the hook
   faces the right way. Rotating the magnet on the steel costs nothing.

</Steps>

## What it will actually hold

The 40 lb on the magnet is **straight pull**, measured square to a thick, clean,
flat steel plate. Nothing about that describes this hook.

On a vertical surface the load is shear, and a magnet in shear is holding on by
friction rather than by magnetism — which is exactly why this one is
rubber-coated, and why what you get is a fraction of the rated pull. Then the
ledge puts the load up to 45 mm out from the face, and that leverage tries to
peel one edge of the magnet off the steel, which is the direction it is weakest
in. Thin sheet, paint, powder coat, a curved surface or a patch of rust each take
another bite.

Load it and find out where it lets go, somewhere the thing falling does not
matter. Cords, bags, headphones, a broom, a hi-vis vest: fine. Anything you would
be upset to hear hit the floor: use two, or use a screw.

## Two things that will bite you

**Both fits are drawn at nominal.** The bore is 6.0 mm for a 6.0 mm stud and the
pocket is 10.0 mm for a 10.0 mm nut. In CAD that is correct; out of a printer it
is interference on both counts, because a printed hole always finishes under
size. Ream and file rather than reprinting at 101% — scaling the part moves the
arch and the mouth with it.

**The stud has to be long enough to reach the nut.** The nut sits 5 mm above the
pad face, so the first 5 mm of any stud is doing nothing but passing through a
clearance hole. There is room for 16 mm of stud in the bore, and the more of it
that ends up inside the nut the better this holds. If yours is short, pack the
nut down onto the floor of the pocket with a thin washer under it.
