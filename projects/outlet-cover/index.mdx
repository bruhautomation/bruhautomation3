Half the reason a room never gets automated is that the lamp is plugged into a
wall outlet and nobody wants a smart plug sticking out of it. This is the other
answer: a relay goes behind the outlet, the brass tab on the receptacle gets
snapped off, and the top socket becomes a Home Assistant switch while the bottom
one stays live for the vacuum. The printed cover is what carries the relay,
because a US wall box that already holds a receptacle and two cables has nowhere
to put one.

:::danger
This is line-voltage work inside a wall box. Kill the breaker, prove it dead
with a tester, and if any part of that sentence is unfamiliar, stop and get an
electrician — or plug a [smart plug](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07BTLHWFM?tag=brau01-20)
into the outlet instead and get 90% of the same result with none of the risk.
:::

## How it works

A duplex receptacle is two sockets that only look independent. The two brass
screws on the hot side are joined by a small break-off tab, and so are the two
silver screws on the neutral side. Snap the **brass** tab and you have two
electrically separate sockets sharing one body — which is exactly how a builder
wires a half-hot bedroom outlet to a wall switch.

Here the wall switch is a WiFi relay. Line feeds the relay, the relay's output
feeds the top socket, and a pigtail off the same line feeds the bottom socket
directly. Nothing about the outlet changes from the front except that one of its
two sockets now answers to Home Assistant.

The relay does not go in the box. A 1-gang box with a receptacle and 14/2 in it
is already at its fill, and if it is steel it is also a Faraday cage that will
sit the relay's antenna at two bars for the rest of its life. The printed cover
stands the whole module off the wall in plastic, in front of the drywall, where
there is room and where the radio works.

**Don't use a bare relay module for this.** A 5 V hobby relay board switches

printed cover, screwed to a wall, in a house — no. The Shelly is the part on
this list because it arrives in its own moulded case with the terminals
recessed, and that case is the insulation. Leave it on.
:::

## Print settings

The cover carries a relay, spans an outlet and is held on by two screws into
the receptacle's yoke, so it is a structural part in the warmest spot in the
room's wiring.

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Perimeters | 4 |
| Infill | 40% |
| Supports | None, printed face down |
| Material | PETG |
| Orientation | Visible face on the bed |

Print it with the face that ends up looking at the room on the build plate. That
gets the one surface anyone sees off the smooth sheet for free and puts the
screw holes vertical, so nothing needs support.

**PETG, not PLA.** PLA goes soft around 60 °C and creeps under a steady load
long before that. This part sits over live terminals and a relay that makes its
own heat, on a wall that may get afternoon sun, and the failure mode is a cover
that slowly sags off the screws. Neither plastic is flame-rated either way,
which is the other half of the caution above: the printed part is a mechanical
cover, not an electrical enclosure. The Shelly's own housing is the enclosure.

## Wiring

| Conductor | Goes to |
|---|---|
| Line (black), from the cable feeding the box | Relay supply live, plus a pigtail to the receptacle's **lower** brass screw |
| Neutral (white) | Relay neutral, plus a pigtail to the receptacle's silver screws |
| Ground (bare or green) | Receptacle ground screw, and the box if it is metal |
| Relay switched output | Receptacle's **upper** brass screw |
| Brass tab between the two brass screws | Snapped off |
| Silver tab between the two silver screws | Left alone |

Terminal markings differ between Shelly generations, so read the diagram printed
on the module you actually bought rather than copying a photo off the internet.
Every one of them has the same three things: a supply pair, a switched output,
and an input for a physical wall switch that this build leaves empty.

If the box is mid-run — two cables in it rather than one — the incoming line,
the outgoing line and the relay's supply all land on the same pigtail. Only the
receptacle's top half comes off the relay output. Get that wrong and you have
put every outlet downstream behind the relay, which you will discover the first
time you turn the lamp off and the television goes with it.

## Assembly

<Steps>

1. **Kill the breaker and prove it.** Test both sockets, both slots, then test
   again once the receptacle is out of the box. A duplex fed from two different
   circuits is rare and it is exactly the thing that catches people.

2. **Pull the receptacle and photograph the box before anything moves.** Count
   the cables. One means you are at the end of the run and nothing is downstream
   of you. Two or more means other outlets depend on this box staying live.

3. **Snap the brass tab.** Grip it with needle-nose pliers and rock it until it
   breaks off. **Brass only.** Break the silver one and the switched socket has
   no return path — it will be dead, it will look like a relay fault, and it
   will cost you an evening.

4. **Wire it to the table above,** on the screw terminals, not the back-stabs.
   Wrap the terminals in a turn of tape before anything folds back in.

5. **Dry-fit everything before you commit.** Relay in the cover, conductors
   folded, cover offered up to the wall. This is the step that tells you whether
   the cover is deep enough for the module you bought, and it is much easier to
   find out now. If the cover has to be pulled tight to close, stop — it is
   levering on live terminals to do it.

6. **Screw the cover on** with the 20 mm 6-32 screws. Snug, then stop; the last
   quarter turn is what splits a printed boss.

7. **Breaker on, lamp test.** The bottom socket should be live immediately. The
   top one should be dead until the relay is switched — press the button on the
   module itself before you involve any software, so you know the wiring is
   right independently of the network.

8. **Join it to WiFi and label the switched socket.** A strip of tape with
   "smart" on it saves the next person in the house from unplugging the router
   into it.

</Steps>

## In Home Assistant

The **Shelly** integration finds it on the network by itself, talks to it
locally, and gives you a `switch` entity plus power and energy sensors from the
metering. There is no cloud in that path and no reason to reflash the module —
the stock firmware is already local.

The metering is what makes this better than a dumb relay. A socket that reports
watts can automate on what is plugged into it rather than on a clock: a curling
iron that has drawn nothing for ten minutes gets turned off, a washing machine
that drops under 5 W has finished, a space heater that has been pulling 1500 W
for three hours gets a notification. Name the entity after the socket rather
than the room, because rooms grow second outlets.

Set the relay's power-on default to **on** in its own settings. If the power
blinks while you are away, the failure you want is a lamp that came back, not a
socket that quietly stayed dead.

:::caution
Nothing goes on the switched half that must not lose power. No fridge, no
freezer, no sump pump, no medical device, no aquarium heater, no network gear
you would need to fix it remotely. An outlet that can be switched off from a
phone is a hazard to anything that assumes it can't be — and everyone in the
house gets to press that button, including the person who doesn't know what it
does.

Skip this on a GFCI receptacle entirely. Its line and load terminals are not
interchangeable with a normal duplex, and putting a relay between them defeats
the protection you were required to have there in the first place. Kitchens,
bathrooms, garages, basements and anything outdoors — leave those alone.
:::

## Troubleshooting

**Both sockets switch together.** The brass tab is still intact, or you have
landed the relay output on both brass screws. Pull it out and look at the tab.

**The switched socket is dead all the time.** Either it is on the relay's supply
input instead of its output, or the silver tab broke while you were working on
the brass one. Both look identical from the front.

**The rest of the room goes off with the lamp.** You put the downstream line
conductor behind the relay. Pigtail so the relay only ever feeds this
receptacle's top half.

**The relay keeps dropping off WiFi.** A steel box is a radio shield, and this
is precisely what the printed cover exists to solve. If the module ended up
tucked into the box because it fit, move it into the cover.

**The cover won't sit flat against the wall.** Stock-length screws, or too much
wire folded behind. Fold the conductors in a flat concertina behind the
receptacle rather than a loop, and check you are using the 20 mm screws.
