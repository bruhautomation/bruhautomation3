A bowl with a rounded foot only ever touches the counter in one small circle, so
anything you do one-handed makes it move. Lean a spoon on the rim and it slides;
whisk in it and it walks; scrape the last of something out of it and it lifts and
goes over. This is the ring that fixes it — the bowl's foot drops into a cradle,
the cradle grips the counter, and nothing moves until you pick the bowl up.

## Material

**PETG.** This lives on a kitchen counter, which means it will meet a bowl that
has just come out of the microwave and it will get rinsed under a hot tap. PLA
starts going soft somewhere around 55–60 °C, and a part that softens under a
loaded bowl deforms into exactly the shape that stops holding it. PETG shrugs
that off, and it is tougher on a drop, which matters for something that lives
near the edge of a counter.

Print the prototypes in PLA+ by all means — it is cheaper, it prints faster, and
you only need it to check the fit. Print the one you keep in PETG.

Skip carbon-filled filament here. It looks good and it is stiffer than you need,
but it is abrasive, and this part rubs a ceramic foot against its bore several
times a day.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls | 4 perimeters |
| Infill | 25% |
| Supports | None |
| Material | PETG |
| Orientation | Flat on the plate, bore vertical |

Flat on the plate is not a preference, it is the only orientation that makes
sense: it puts every layer line horizontal, so the load from the bowl presses
layers together instead of trying to peel them apart, and it needs no supports
because nothing overhangs. Print it on the textured side of the plate if your
printer has one — the bottom face is a grip surface, and a textured first layer
is free grip before you add a single pad.

The walls matter more than the infill. Four perimeters on a ring is nearly a
solid part already; pushing infill past 25% costs print time and buys nothing.

## Sizing it to your bowl

This is the whole design. There is one dimension that matters and it is in your
cupboard, not in this repo.

<Steps>

1. **Measure the foot** — the ring the bowl actually stands on — across its
   outside diameter with the caliper. Do it on the biggest bowl in the set and
   the smallest.

2. **Add clearance to the bore.** 0.3–0.5 mm on the diameter is the usual FDM
   fit for a part you want to drop in without aiming. Tighter than that and a wet
   bowl binds; looser and it rattles.

3. **Make the bore a shallow cone, not a cylinder.** Widen it a degree or two
   towards the top and one rest catches every bowl in the cupboard instead of one
   of them. A cone self-centres; a cylinder either fits or it doesn't.

4. **Keep it low.** The rest only has to catch the foot. A tall wall hides the
   bowl, makes it awkward to lift out with wet hands, and turns a discreet ring
   into a piece of furniture.

5. **Print one and try it** before you print the set. Fit is the only thing that
   can go wrong on this part, and it is a twenty-minute print to check.

</Steps>

## Grip

The rest is only as good as its bottom face. A printed ring on a wiped counter
slides as happily as the bowl did, and then you have moved the problem rather
than solved it.

Three pads, not four. Three always sit flat; four rock on any counter that isn't
perfectly flat, which is all of them. Space them evenly around the underside and
keep them in from the outer edge so nobody can catch one and peel it off.

TPU printed in place is the tidier answer if you have a direct-drive printer and
don't mind the filament change — pause at the right layer, swap, carry on. The
adhesive bumpers are the honest one: they cost six dollars for two hundred, they
grip better than most TPU does, and if one lets go you replace it in a second.
Wipe the print with alcohol before sticking them down, because PETG straight off
the plate carries enough finger oil to beat the adhesive.

## Finishing

Knock the elephant's foot off the first layer with the deburring tool and take it
back flush. That flare sits exactly where the bowl's foot lands, and it is the
difference between a bowl that drops in and a bowl you have to press in.

Then run the bore with sandpaper until it is smooth. It is a bearing surface for
a glazed ceramic foot, and a rough bore both grinds at the glaze and holds
whatever was last in the bowl.

:::caution
This is not a food-contact part, and no FDM print is. Layer lines are a texture
that traps food and cannot be scrubbed clean, so the rest touches the *outside*
of the bowl and nothing else.

Hand-wash it, and keep it out of the dishwasher — a dry cycle runs hot enough to
distort PETG, and it will lift the adhesive off every pad on the bottom. Don't
put a pan straight off the hob on it either.
:::

## Troubleshooting

**The bowl rattles in it.** The bore is too big. Rescale the model to 99.5% —
uniformly, in all three axes — and reprint. Scaling one axis makes the bore an
oval, and an oval grips in one position and flops in the other.

**The bowl won't drop in.** Either the bore is too tight or the elephant's foot
is still there. Check the bottom edge first, because that is the fault nine times
in ten and it takes thirty seconds to fix.

**The whole rest slides.** That is the pads, not the design. Either they have
picked up oil or flour, or they were stuck to a print that was never wiped down.
Clean both faces with alcohol and stick fresh ones on.

**It has gone soft or sits crooked.** It was printed in PLA and it met something
hot. Reprint in PETG.
