Magnetic cabinet locks are the good kind of childproofing — nothing screwed to
the outside of the door, nothing for a toddler to chew on, and the cabinet still
looks like a cabinet. Then they hand you one key: a plastic puck that lives on
top of the fridge, three rooms from the cupboard you are trying to open, while
you are holding a kid in one arm and a saucepan in the other. This is that key,
printed as a ring, so it is already on the hand that reaches for the door.

## What actually has to happen

The lock is a spring-loaded steel latch screwed to the inside of the cabinet
door. Bring a strong enough magnet to the outside of the door front and the latch
is pulled clear of its catch; take the magnet away and the spring puts it back.
There is no mechanism, no code and no fit — the key never touches the lock.

So the whole project is one question: **does your magnet swing that latch through
your door front?** A cabinet door is typically around 3/4 in of wood between the
magnet and the latch, and magnetic field falls off fast over that distance. The
ring is just a comfortable way to hold a magnet that already works. Sort out the
magnet first and print second.

## Picking the magnet

The factory key in the lock box is a plain disc about an inch across with a steel
plate behind it. The steel is not decoration: backing a magnet with steel pushes
more of its field out the front, which is exactly the trick you want when the
target is on the far side of 3/4 in of door.

A cup magnet is the same idea taken one step further, and that step costs you
something. The steel wraps the sides as well as the back, so it hits like nothing
else on contact — 110 lb of it — but the flux loops home through the rim, and the
reach at distance is shorter than a bare disc of the same diameter. It is still
by far the strongest, widest thing in most parts drawers, and diameter is what
gets you through a door.

**Test it before you model anything.** Hold the bare magnet flat against the
outside of a locked door, over where the latch sits, and slide it around. If the
latch drops, you have your key and the rest is printing. If it doesn't, no ring
design will fix that, and what you want instead is a plain uncupped
[neodymium disc around an inch across](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GQ3GB3RQ?tag=brau01-20).

Two things that follow from the cup:

**Fit it open face out.** Only the face the magnet shows works. Mount it backwards
and the ring is a steel washer on your finger.

**Small discs are not a substitute.** Stacking
[6 × 3 mm discs](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07873ZCY4?tag=brau01-20) makes a
taller magnet, not a longer-reaching one — at these distances the diameter of the
pole face is what counts, and 6 mm of it is nothing. A stack will pop a thin
drawer front and will not touch a proper door. They are useful for the mock-up
you print to check the band size, and that is about it.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.15 mm |
| Walls / perimeters | 4 or more |
| Infill | 40% |
| Supports | None |
| Material | PETG, or PLA+ indoors |
| Orientation | Band flat on the plate, magnet pocket opening upward |

**Lay the band flat on the plate.** Then the hoop of the ring is one continuous
extrusion going round and round, and the load a ring actually sees — being pulled
off a finger, being spread to get it on — pulls along the extrusions instead of
across the joints between layers. Stand it up on edge and the band becomes a
stack of layer joints in exactly the plane it wants to split on. It will last
until the first time you catch it on a drawer handle.

Flat also puts the magnet pocket opening upward, so it slices as a plain vertical
hole with nothing to bridge and no supports anywhere in the part.

Walls do the work at this size — four perimeters on a 0.4 mm nozzle is most of a
thin band solid, and the infill percentage barely gets a say.

## Assembly

<Steps>

1. **Measure the magnet.** Diameter and thickness with the caliper, and the
   countersink too if you are going to screw through it. Do this before you draw
   the pocket rather than after, because a magnet that does not sit flush stands
   the ring off the door and costs you the distance you were fighting for.

2. **Measure the finger you will wear it on**, and print the band on its own once
   before you print the whole thing. A ring is a part where half a millimetre is
   the difference between wearing it all day and taking it off after an hour.

3. **Open the pocket up.** A printed hole always finishes under size, so if the
   magnet does not drop in under thumb pressure, take the walls back with a
   needle file rather than reprinting the ring 2% larger — scaling moves the
   finger bore with it.

4. **Fit the magnet open face out**, screw it down through the countersink if the
   design takes the screw, and put a drop of CA around the seat either way.

5. **Break the inside edge of the band** with the deburring tool. This is the
   step people skip and then wonder why they never wear it.

6. **Test it on the cabinet you built it for.** Then hang off it — pull the magnet
   hard, twist it, knock the ring against the counter. Whatever comes off in your
   hand now is better than it coming off on the kitchen floor later.

</Steps>

:::danger
**Neodymium magnets are a surgical emergency if a child swallows two of them.**
They find each other through the wall of the gut and pinch it closed. This is a
project you are building because there is a small person in the house, and it
puts a very strong magnet within their reach by design, so treat the bond between
magnet and ring as the safety-critical part of the build: screw it, glue it, and
check it now and then. The rest of the pack goes somewhere they cannot get to —
not a drawer, not a bowl by the door.
:::

:::caution
A 110 lb magnet is not a toy in the other direction either. It will jump the last
centimetre to any steel it can see and it does not care what is between — a
fingertip against a fridge door or a tool chest is a genuine blood blister. Slide
it onto steel from the side rather than dropping it on. Keep it away from cards
with a stripe, mechanical drives, watches, hearing aids and pacemakers, and
remember it is on your hand now, so it goes wherever your hand goes.
:::

## The thing nobody tells you about magnetic locks

The lock is only as childproof as the key is out of reach. The whole design
assumes an adult carries the key and a child cannot find it. Wear the ring and
that is exactly what happens. Leave it on the counter beside the cupboard it
opens and you have spent an evening printing yourself a slightly worse fridge
puck.
