A label comes off a jar in one piece or it comes off in forty, and the
difference is almost never how hard you scrape. It is temperature, the angle you
pull at, and whether the thing touching the container is steel or plastic. This
is the printed holder that fixes the blade at that angle and keeps your thumb
behind the edge, plus the small kit of consumables that does the actual work.

Reusing jars is the reason to build it. Spice jars, pasta sauce jars, reagent
bottles, a growler you want to relabel — all of them come with a label somebody
expected to stay on forever, and all of them are worth more clean than they are
in the recycling.

## Steel on glass, plastic on everything else

This is the one decision that ruins containers, so make it first.

**Glass takes the steel blade.** Nothing you can do with a single-edge razor at a
shallow angle marks annealed glass, and steel is the only edge that will get
under old, hardened adhesive without flexing away from it.

**Everything else takes the plastic blade.** HDPE bottles, PET, painted tins,
powder-coated lids, anodised aluminium, acrylic — a steel blade scratches all of
them, and on a curved surface it doesn't take much pressure to skate off the
label and into the container. Or your thumb. The plastic blades are effectively
free at 100 to a pack, they dull in about one bottle, and you throw them away
rather than push a blunt one harder.

:::caution
The blade only ever moves away from your hand. A jar is a curved, wet, slippery
thing to hold, and the moment the blade breaks free of the label it accelerates.
Hold the container against the bench rather than in mid-air, and keep the hand
that isn't holding the tool behind the edge, not in front of it.
:::

## Heat

Warm, not hot. The adhesive under a pressure-sensitive label goes soft and
stringy long before anything else on the jar changes, and the target is the point
where the glass is uncomfortable to keep hold of — not the point where it's
smoking. Set the gun low, keep it moving, hold it a hand's width back, and warm
the whole label rather than the corner you're working on.

:::danger
**Never park a heat gun on one spot of cold glass.** That is thermal shock, and a
jar that cracks in your hand is doing it while you have a razor blade in the
other one. Bring the whole container up together, and don't take a jar straight
out of a cold garage and heat it.

**Don't heat plastic containers at all.** PET starts to shrink and go oval well
below the temperature that softens the adhesive, and once a bottle has drawn in
it never comes back. Plastic gets the soak and the solvent, not the gun.
:::

The soak is the alternative and it costs you nothing but time: hot tap water, a
squeeze of dish soap, twenty minutes fully submerged. The paper comes away on its
own and you go straight to the residue. Heat is what you use when the label is
the plasticised, water-proof kind that laughs at a soak, or when you have thirty
jars and no patience.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls | 4 |
| Infill | 40% |
| Supports | None |
| Material | PETG |
| Orientation | Layers running along the blade slot, never across it |

**PETG, because of the heat gun.** PLA goes soft somewhere around 60 °C and a
heat gun on its low setting blows straight past that; the first time you rest the
holder against a jar you have just warmed, a PLA one takes a set and the blade
stops sitting square. PETG buys you another 20 °C or so, which is the whole
margin you need. Neither will survive being pointed at directly, so don't.

Orientation is the other thing that will bite you. A slot printed across the
layers splits along a layer line the first time you lever with it, and levering
is the entire load case of this tool. Lay the part down so the layers run the
length of whatever holds the blade, and put the walls up to four so the pocket
has some meat around it — this is a part where extra perimeters matter far more
than extra infill.

## The method

<Steps>

1. **Pick the blade for the surface.** Steel on glass, plastic on everything
   else. Snap a fresh one in; a blade that has done a jar is already dull.

2. **Warm the label** — whole thing, gun moving, hand's width back — or soak the
   container in hot soapy water for twenty minutes. Skip both if the label is
   plain paper and already lifting at a corner.

3. **Get under one corner** with the blade almost flat to the surface. If you are
   fighting it, the label isn't warm enough. Do not solve it with a steeper
   angle.

4. **Pull the label back over itself**, close to 180°, and keep going slowly.
   Peel force climbs steeply with the angle: fold it back on itself and paper
   that would tear at 45° comes away whole. Slow and continuous beats fast and
   stuttering every time.

5. **Follow the peel with the blade**, not the other way round. The blade's job
   is to break the adhesive line just ahead of where the paper is already
   lifting. Leading with it is what shreds labels.

6. **Deal with the residue** while the container is still warm — see below. It
   never gets easier than it is at this moment.

</Steps>

## Residue

The paper was never the problem. What's left is a film of adhesive, and it comes
off with dwell time rather than force.

Spray it with the 99% isopropyl, leave it 30 to 60 seconds, then work it into a
ball with a microfibre cloth. It rolls up on itself and lifts. Re-wet and repeat
rather than scrubbing dry — dry scrubbing smears it into a haze that then takes
three times as long.

On glass you can escalate: a plastic blade to shear the softened film off, and
acetone if the alcohol won't touch it. **On plastic, alcohol and nothing
stronger.** Acetone crazes PET and polystyrene on contact, and a hazy, crackled
bottle is worse than one with a label on it.

## Troubleshooting

**The label tears into confetti.** Too cold, or you are pulling at too steep an
angle. Both are fixed by the same thing — more warmth, flatter pull, slower hand.

**The jar cracked.** The gun was held still on cold glass. Warm the whole
container, keep the gun moving, and never start on a jar that's been out in the
cold.

**The bottle went oval.** That was plastic and it got heat. There is no fixing
it; use the soak on the next one.

**The blade skates off the label.** Either the angle is too steep or the blade is
blunt. Blades are consumables and a dull one is what makes people push harder,
which is exactly how a steel blade ends up somewhere it shouldn't be.

**A ghost of adhesive the alcohol won't shift.** That is a rubber-based glue
rather than an acrylic one. Give it a long soak in hot soapy water first, or lay
an alcohol-soaked cloth over it and leave it five minutes — the solvent needs to
get through the whole thickness, and it can only do that with time.
