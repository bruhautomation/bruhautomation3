A bench covered in twelve-place tube racks is a bench where half the racks are
somewhere else. This is one printed slab with enough positions to hold an entire
run of 15 mL conicals in order, in one thing you can pick up with one hand. It
prints in one piece, so there is nothing to assemble, nothing to glue and nothing
to lose in a drawer.

- [Deburring tool](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09ND84SXF?tag=brau01-20) — one pass round the top of each bore; this is the difference between a tube dropping in and a tube being screwed in
- [Self-adhesive rubber bumpers](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B088HGQSZT?tag=brau01-20) — four under the corners so a loaded rack doesn't skate on a wiped-down bench (200 pcs, you need four)

- [CA glue with activator](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0849YVVSL?tag=brau01-20) — only if the footprint is bigger than your bed and you cut it in the slicer

## Before you print

Every 15 mL conical is about 17 mm across the barrel and about 120 mm long, and
it hangs in a rack by its cap, not by its body — the cap is wider than the
barrel, and that lip is the whole retention mechanism. Brands vary by a few
tenths of a millimetre. Put a caliper on the tubes in your own store cupboard
before you start a print that is going to cut sixty of those holes.

If the bores come out wrong, fix them in the model, not with the slicer's scale
box. Scaling the part scales the pitch, the wall thickness and the deck
thickness along with the hole, and you will end up with a rack that fits the
tubes and no longer fits the bench.

## Print settings

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls / perimeters | 3 |
| Infill | 15% |
| Supports | None |
| Material | PETG |
| Orientation | Flat on the bed, bores vertical |

Bores vertical is the only sensible orientation — every hole is then a vertical
tube the printer bridges nothing across, and the part needs no supports at all.

Turn up wall count rather than infill. Fifteen millilitres of water is 15 grams,
and a megarack holds a lot of tubes, so the load on the deck adds up quickly.
Perimeters carry that; sparse infill in the middle of a thin deck does very
little.

## Material

**PETG.** A tube rack lives on a bench next to things that are warm — a
waterbath, a bead bath, an incubator door, a sunny window over a summer — and
PLA starts going soft around 60 °C. A rack that sags is a rack whose bores no
longer line up. PETG holds its shape through all of that and shrugs off being
dropped, which is the other thing that happens to tube racks.

PLA+ is fine if you know the rack stays cool, and it prints more accurately, so
the bores come out closer to the number in the model.

:::caution
Do not autoclave a printed rack. An autoclave cycle runs at 121 °C and both PETG
and PLA are well past soft by then — you will open the chamber to find a
puddle. Printed racks are bench organisers, not sterile consumables: the layer
lines hold liquid that you cannot get back out of them. Wipe it down with 70%
ethanol and keep it out of the autoclave and the glasswasher.
:::

## Finishing

Run the deburring tool once round the mouth of every bore. Fresh off the bed,
the first layer of each hole carries a little elephant's foot and the top edge
carries a burr, and both of them catch on the tube's cap skirt. A quarter turn
each and a tube drops in under its own weight — which is what you want when you
are loading a rack one-handed with a pipette in the other.

Four rubber bumpers under the corners next. A full rack is heavy and the bench
is usually damp; without feet it will slide the moment you pull a tube.

Then label it. A rack with numbered rows is a rack you can write positions down
against, and the laminated tape survives an ethanol wipe where a marker line
does not.
